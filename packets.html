PACKET 1

Introduction to Media and Information Literacy

Understand literacy, communication, and the different types of literacy.

Learning objectives

  • Define Media and Information Literacy (MIL).
  • Identify the communication model.
  • Differentiate media, information, technology, and digital literacy.

Lesson discussion

Media and Information Literacy (MIL) refers to competencies—knowledge, skills, and attitudes—that allow people to engage with media and other information. These competencies include accessing, evaluating, creating, sharing, and communicating effectively.

Literacy commonly describes the ability to read, write, speak, listen, understand, and communicate effectively.

Communication model

Sender → Message → Channel → Receiver, with encoding and decoding involved. Feedback lets the sender know how the message was received.

Data to wisdom

Data are raw facts; information is organized data; knowledge is understanding gained from information; wisdom is using knowledge with good judgment.

Four types of literacy

  • Media literacy: the ability to access, analyze, evaluate, identify, and produce communication in different media forms.
  • Information literacy: recognizing when information is needed and locating, evaluating, organizing, using, and communicating it.
  • Technology literacy: using appropriate technology tools safely, responsibly, creatively, and effectively.
  • Digital literacy: responsibly using digital technology, communication tools, and networks to locate, evaluate, use, and create information.
Example: Checking an official school page before believing a post about class cancellations shows evaluation of a media source. Comparing several sources for a research task demonstrates information literacy.

PACKET 2

Evolution of Media

Learn how communication and media changed over time.

Learning objectives

  • Identify traditional and new media.
  • Recognize examples of media from different ages.
  • Describe how media changed communication.

Lesson discussion

Media has changed over time, from signs and early writing systems to printed materials, electronic devices, and internet-based communication.

01

Pre-Industrial Age

Before 1700, people relied on nature and developed early materials and tools. Examples from the lesson include cave paintings, papyrus, and early writing systems.

02

Industrial Age

1700s–1930s in the lesson timeline. Steam power, machine tools, iron production, and mass production changed society. The printing press enabled printed texts to be produced more widely; motion pictures also became a form of media.

03

Electronic Age

1930s–1980s in the lesson timeline. Transistor radios, electronic circuits, early computers, television, and telephones made long-distance communication more efficient.

04

New / Information Age

Associated in the lesson with the internet, faster communication, social networks, and newer digital technologies such as augmented and virtual reality and wearable technology.

Sources of information in the lesson

  • Indigenous media: media expression conceptualized, produced, and circulated by Indigenous people; supports cultural preservation and expression.
  • Library: a place where reference materials such as manuscripts, books, and films are kept for use.
  • Internet media: interconnected computer networks that provide access to varied information. The lesson mentions ARPANET (1969) and the World Wide Web.

PACKET 3

Information Literacy

Learn to recognize information needs, search carefully, evaluate quality, and communicate knowledge ethically.

Learning objectives

  • Define information literacy.
  • Explain why critical thinking matters.
  • Identify the stages of information literacy.

Lesson discussion

Information literacy is the ability to recognize when information is needed, then locate, evaluate, organize, use, and communicate it in different formats. The lesson also emphasizes using information in a way that benefits others.

Critical thinking means questioning, analyzing, interpreting, evaluating, and making judgments about what you read, hear, say, or write instead of automatically accepting claims. It helps people reach reasonable conclusions and make decisions.

Seven stages of information literacy

  1. Identifying information needs: decide whether information is needed to be updated, educated, or entertained. Questions such as who, what, when, where, why, and how help define the need.
  2. Determining sources: decide where to search, such as Indigenous people or community sources, libraries, and internet sources.
  3. Locating / searching information: acquire information through observation, listening, newspapers, television, libraries, museums, and online sources.
  4. Analyzing and evaluating quality: gather facts, apply critical analysis, identify classes of information, recognize logical fallacies, and use fact-checking resources.
  5. Organizing, storing, or archiving: keep information physically or digitally by writing, printing, saving, downloading, or using cloud storage.
  6. Using information and knowledge: apply information to resolve uncertainty or address a need.
  7. Creating and communicating new knowledge: present, apply, and share information that has been gathered and processed.
Example: For a school report, identify the topic you need, choose credible sources, search, compare the information, save useful findings, use them in your report, and cite or communicate them responsibly.

PACKET 4

Media and Information Sources: RAVAT

Evaluate the quality of information and recognize common logical fallacies.

Learning objective

Examine and compare information from different sources to evaluate reliability, accuracy, value, authority, timeliness, and bias.

RAVAT checklist

R - Reliability

Can the information be verified? Is the source trustworthy?

A - Accuracy

Does the report match the actual data? Does the argument flow logically?

V - Value

Does the information meet its purpose to update, educate, entertain, or help improve a decision?

A - Authority

Does the source have established expertise on the subject?

T - Timeliness

Is the information current enough for the topic? Reliability, accuracy, and value can change over time.

Evidence and classes of information

The lesson lists three types of evidence: object, documentary, and testimonial. It also identifies three classes of information: fact-based information, substantiated opinions, and unsubstantiated news and opinions.

Common logical fallacies

  • Ad hominem: attacking a person instead of addressing their argument.
  • Appeal to authority: treating a claim as correct simply because a famous or powerful person supports it.
  • Bandwagon: treating a claim as correct simply because many people believe it.

Ways to check information

  • Check the author and the publication or update date.
  • Look for citations and evidence.
  • Check the domain and the owner of the site or page.
  • Review the site design and writing style.
  • Compare the claim with other reliable sources and fact-checking resources.

The lesson gives .com, .edu, .gov, .org, and .net as examples of domains. A domain alone does not prove that every claim on a site is reliable.

PACKET 5

Media Languages, Codes, Conventions, and Messages

Explore how media creators use technical, symbolic, and written elements to communicate meaning.

Learning objectives

  • Identify different media languages.
  • Differentiate media codes, conventions, audiences, and stakeholders.
  • Recognize producers of media.

Key concepts

  • Languages: technical and symbolic ingredients, codes, and conventions that media professionals select to communicate ideas, information, and knowledge.
  • Media languages: codes, conventions, formats, symbols, and narrative structures that indicate meaning in media messages.
  • Message: information sent from a source to a receiver or audience.
  • Narrative: the telling of a story or plot through a sequence of events.
  • Media conventions: expected ways in which codes are organized in a media product.
  • Media codes: technical, written, and symbolic tools used to construct or suggest meaning in media products.

Three types of media codes

Technical codes

Codes specific to a media form. Examples in the lesson include camera shots, lighting, and sound.

Symbolic codes

Meaning depends on how an audience interprets what is shown, such as symbols and visual signs.

Written codes

Formal written language used to create a media product, including text in frame, typography, and dialogue.

Audience, stakeholders, and producers

  • Audience: the people who receive or watch a media message.
  • Stakeholders: people or organizations with a shared interest or concern related to a group or issue.
  • Producers of media: may include authors, journalists, editors, performers, talents, and directors.
Example: In a short video, camera shots and sound are technical codes; a color or symbol may work as a symbolic code; captions and dialogue are written codes. Together, these choices help shape how the audience understands the message.
